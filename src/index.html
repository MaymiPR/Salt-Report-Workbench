S.A.L.T. Report Workbench User Manual

A quick tour of the things that aren’t obvious at first glance.

What This Tool Does

S.A.L.T. Report Workbench is a locally-run logging tool for SALT reports (Size, Activity, Location, Time). Open the HTML in any browser and it just works: no install, no internet, no server required. Everything you log is saved to your browser’s local storage and stays there until you clear it.

Online sync is optional. When you run the included Python sync server, every connected browser on the network shares the same workspace in real time, and every change gets backed up to disk. Lose connection mid-session and the tool keeps working locally: your changes queue in local storage and replay automatically the moment the server is back.

Report Identity

Your identity is the initials and tag color shown in the By column on every report you log. It lets you (and anyone else watching) see at a glance who logged what.

  • Set your Initials and Tag Color on first launch via the startup modal.
  • The startup modal opens with a compact Quick Start strip listing what it already knows as one-click presets, in three rows: Operator (known initials with their tag color), ATO (loaded workspaces), and Title (report titles seen before). The rows are independent, so you can pick a known operator and ATO but type a brand-new title. Picking an ATO also fills its usual title if you haven’t typed one. Each row shows up to five entries, and the whole strip hides on a first-ever run when there’s nothing to offer.
  • To change them later: click the Manage icon (the folder, top right of the report panel), then click the person icon tab. Edit and press Set Initials.
  • The Identity tab also shows the current Access Code, hidden as dots until you press Show, with a one-click Copy button (handy for sharing with a teammate joining the session).
  • Each device or operator has its own identity stored per-browser.

Locally: Identity stays in your browser only. Your initials and color appear on the reports you log.

With sync server: Identity is also broadcast to the server, so every connected peer sees your initials and color on your reports in their own table.

Set Up Your Workspace

  • Type your ATO Designator (the workspace key, e.g. ALPHA01) and a Title (e.g. Patrol Report) in the top form.
  • Press Set, or just hit Enter from either field. Both fields lock and that ATO becomes the active workspace.
  • To switch workspaces later, press Change. This unlocks the ATO/Title fields and the dropdown below.
  • The dropdown lists every other workspace available. Pick one to jump to it.
  • To create a brand-new workspace, just type a new ATO name and press Set. No separate “new” button needed.

Locally: The ATO/Title pair is remembered per-browser; the dropdown lists every workspace your browser has cached.

With sync server: Each editor has their own workspace: setting or picking one moves only you, and the others keep theirs. The Workspaces tab in Manage shows the initials of the editors in each workspace, and pointing at the connected-users count lists who is where. Viewers are shown the last workspace an editor switched to. If another editor unloads the workspace you are in, you are told, and you set one to keep logging. The dropdown lists every workspace currently loaded on the server.

Log a New Report

  • Fill in Size, Activity, Location (required), plus Time (optional). The Analysis box is hidden by default; enable it per workspace from the Builder tab if you use it.
  • MGRS Lock (Settings menu, per browser; when you update a report, its grids in your zone and square show as their digits, the zone and square staying in the lock's box): set your grid zone and 100 km square once (11S NV, in the box that appears in front of Location) and type just the digits: 39697 06762 is logged as 11S NV 39697 06762. Each comma-separated part that is only an even run of digits gets it; full grids, place names and odd numbers (Hill 205) are left alone. The NAI / OBJ / PL hints work from the full grid as you type. A grid after a lead word (vic 39697 06762; also vicinity of, IVO, near, nr, grid, approx, at) gets the zone and square too, and is checked the same way. A part that looks meant as a grid but isn't one gets an orange Not a grid chip under Location as you type, saying why: digits alone with an odd count (396970676), more than 10, two numbers of different lengths (39697 0676), or, typed in full, a zone, band or square that doesn't exist (38I MB 45000 82000). Log and Update then ask: Fix it goes back to Location, Log as typed logs it as it is (it won't be read as a grid). Place names and numbers with words never get one.
  • Add Nearby Places (Settings menu, per browser, on to start with): with it on, Log and Update add to the Location the NAI or OBJ a grid is in and a phase line within 1 km. Turn it off when you don't want them written: the places still show under Location, in grey and without a +, so you know they're there, and nothing is added.
  • Multiple grids auto-separate: type several MGRS coordinates in Location without commas (12ABC123456 21CBA321654, spaced digits fine too) and commas are inserted between them on log. If you type your own commas, or mix in free text, nothing is changed.
  • Time field: type HHMM and it auto-formats to HH:MM on blur. Leave it blank and the report gets stamped with the current time when you submit. A typed time gets today’s date, unless it is more than an hour ahead of now, in which case it is taken as yesterday’s: in Zulu mode the day rolls over during a US evening, so 23:50 typed at 00:30Z means forty minutes ago.
  • Observer (with FMV mode on): the drone the report was seen from, one of those on station, or No observer. Your pick stays for your next report while that drone is on station. It is saved on the report and its earlier versions, shown small under the report’s time, in its card on the map, and in the CSV export’s last column; it isn’t in the copied SALT text. A report being edited shows its own observer, even one since taken off station or removed.
  • Press the blue Log New Report button or hit Enter from any field.
  • After submission: the report auto-copies to your clipboard in proper SALT format, ready to paste into a chat or radio script.
  • Numbering is automatic (highest existing + 1). Override using the small Number box beside New Report.
  • Esc while filling out a new report clears all the fields.

Locally: The report saves to your browser’s local storage immediately and stays there until you clear it.

With sync server: The report is also pushed to the server and broadcast to every connected peer in real time. If the server is unreachable mid-log, the report queues in local storage and replays the moment the server is back.

Edit, Update, and Restore an Existing Report

Click any row to load it into the form. The submit area swaps the single Log button for an Edit + Update pair.

To Update a report (the situation actually changed)

  1. Click the row in the table.
  2. Change the fields. Press the Now button to stamp the update with the current time, or type a new Time manually. Leave the Time box as it is and the report keeps its original timestamp, date included.
  3. Press Update (blue) or hit Enter.

The old values snapshot into the report’s history, the report gets an (Updated) badge in the table, and the new SALT auto-copies to your clipboard with (Updated) appended to the identifier line.

To Edit a report (typo or formatting fix)

  1. Click the row in the table.
  2. Fix the field(s).
  3. Press Edit (gray) or hit Ctrl+Enter.

The report is overwritten in place: no history entry, no (Updated) badge. Use when you’re correcting yourself, not recording a real change.

To Restore a previous version (rollback)

  1. Click the chevron (›) next to the row to expand its history.
  2. Find the version you want to bring back.
  3. Click the green Restore pill and confirm.

That historical version becomes the current state. Destructive: the current values and every history entry newer than the chosen one are discarded. Older entries are preserved. The confirmation message offers Undo for ten seconds, which brings the report back exactly as it was.

What the buttons do:

  • Update (blue): pushes the current values into history and marks the report (Updated).
  • Edit (gray): overwrites in place. No history entry, no (Updated) badge.
  • Now: snaps the Time field to the current time. Only active while editing an existing report.
  • Restore (green pill on a history sub-row): destructive rollback to that historical version.

Shortcuts while editing:

  • Enter: Update
  • Ctrl+Enter: Edit
  • Esc: Cancel and clear the form

Locally: All three operations persist to your browser’s local storage immediately.

With sync server: Each operation is also pushed to the server; the server rewrites the backup file and broadcasts to peers so everyone sees the change instantly. Restore’s discards are reflected in the backup file too: the rolled-back entries don’t survive.

Working with the Table

  • Click any row to load it into the form for editing (or Tab to its Identifier and press Enter). If someone else changes it before you press Edit or Update, you’re asked whether to keep theirs or overwrite it.
  • Copy button on each row copies that report to clipboard in SALT format. If the report has been updated, the identifier line includes (Updated) so the recipient knows it’s a revised entry, not the original.
  • Chevron (›) next to a report expands its history below: every prior version with size, activity, location, time, and who saved it.
  • (Updated) tag under the ID means the report has been changed at least once.
  • Checkboxes select rows; the Delete button appears at the top once anything is checked. Works on top-level reports AND individual history rows. Esc deselects everything at once. Delete asks first, on the button itself: the first press turns it into Delete 2 for everyone? (with the count checked), and a second press deletes; changing what is checked, or pressing any other button, lets the question go. After a delete, the message at the bottom carries an Undo button for ten seconds that puts everything back (except a Not saved change, which is discarded for good).
  • Removing anything works the same way: reports, BDA assessments, overlays, basemaps, drones, narrative settings and workspaces. The first press asks … for everyone? where you pressed, on the button or in the name beside it, so nothing moves; a second press removes it; any other button lets the question go. The message that follows has an Undo button for ten seconds (it waits while the pointer is on it), which puts back what was removed, for everyone.
  • Dense cells stay compact: Size, Activity, Location, and Analysis are each capped at two lines. When content overflows (several MGRS grids, a wordy analysis), that cell turns clickable; click to expand it in place, click again to collapse. Cells whose content already fits stay plain and are not clickable. Hovering shows the full content as a tooltip, and it works on history rows too.
  • Sort by clicking a column header. Identifier cycles number ascending / descending / most recently changed (the default); Time, Size, Activity, and Location each toggle ascending / descending. Text columns sort with numbers in order, so 2x T-72 comes before 10x T-72.
  • Select All / Deselect All in the Settings (gear) menu hit every visible checkbox at once.

Locally: Sort, history toggle, copy, row selection, and clicking-to-edit all run entirely in your browser.

With sync server: Deletions also remove the entries from the server’s in-memory store and rewrite the backup file. Other connected browsers see the deletion within milliseconds.

Search Local only

Search runs entirely in your browser. It filters the rows already loaded; it never queries the server.

  • Ctrl+S jumps straight into the search bar.
  • Esc while focused on the search bar clears the text and the filter. Table snaps back to the full view.
  • General typing matches across ID, size, activity, location, analysis, reporter initials, the formatted time, any custom builder fields, and BDA assessments (target, coords, effect).
  • Cardinal direction shortcut: type a single direction token (n, s, e, w, ne, nw, se, or sw) and the search switches to a word-boundary match on Activity. So se finds “moving SE” without also matching the “se” inside “observed”.

The Four Icons

At the top right of the report panel, a square of four icons is there in every view:

  • Folder: Manage (import and export, loaded workspaces, the report builder, the map's graphics and basemaps, your identity).
  • Bar chart: Statistics. Page: Summary Reports.
  • Gear: Settings: layout, Zulu or Local time, the title separator, BDA, MGRS Lock, Add Nearby Places, Units, FMV mode, Select All / Deselect All, the User Manual, and Clear Workspace, which unloads your workspace: it asks Unload ALPHA for everyone? (your workspace's name) in its place, and the menu stays open for a second press.
  • The icon of the view on screen is blue; click it again to go back to the Report Log (or the map under it, if that was on screen).
  • The map is under the Report Log's table: the map button beside the Report Log heading slides down to it, and the same button (a list by then) slides back. The bar stays put, and its search narrows the map too.

Time & Layout Toggles Local only

Layout and time-format choices are saved per-browser and never sync. Every operator can pick their own.

  • Zulu / Local Time (in the Settings menu): flips every visible time stamp between UTC (Z) and your browser’s local time (L). The column header shows which mode you’re in. The actual stored times never change; only the display does.
  • Vertical layout (default): form on the left, table on the right. The page itself doesn’t scroll; only the table scrolls inside its panel.
  • Horizontal layout: a compact form across the top (the fields and Log on one row, Analysis under them when your format has it) and the report panel filling the rest of the window. Only the table scrolls; on a short window the page scrolls instead of squeezing it.
  • Title Separator (in the Settings menu): switches the character between the report title and the ATO designator everywhere the identifier is displayed: workspace lists, the table’s Identifier column, and copied report text. Default is a dash (Patrol Report - ALPHA001); toggle to a plain space (Patrol Report ALPHA001). Display-only; stored report data never changes.
  • Units: Metric / Imperial (in the Settings menu; the button says which is on): how distances, areas and speeds read, on the map’s measure tools, its track hovers, a report’s rate of march, and the drones’ ranges and distances (a range is typed in the same units, km or miles, and kept in km for everyone). Metric: km (m when short), dismounts in m, aircraft in km, areas in km² (m² when small), speeds in km/h. Imperial: miles (yards when short), dismounts in yards, aircraft in nautical miles, areas in mi² (yd² when small), speeds in mph, and knots for aircraft. Bearings stay in degrees with the compass point. Display-only, per browser; drone ranges are still typed in km.
  • On narrow screens the two panels stack vertically and the page scrolls; nothing is hidden or disabled, just rearranged.

Manage

Open with the Manage icon (the folder). The report panel shows five tabs: Import / Export, Workspaces, Builder (see Report Builder below), Map (two tabs: Graphics, the ops graphics, see Overlays and ArcGIS below, and Basemaps, see The Map), and Identity. Viewers get only Import / Export, for exporting. A tab you pick slides in from its side (not with your system's reduce motion setting); so do the Map tab's two, the Summary Reports' kinds and the Narrative settings' tabs.

Import / Export tab (default)

  • Export and Import are on the same screen. Export: pick what from the pop-up menu (grouped as Reports, ArcGIS points and ArcGIS tracks, the picked one checked; the arrow keys, Enter and a format's first letter work too; a line under it says what it is and how to use it) and press Export. Below it is the Time range; beside that, the Options for the picked format (when it has any), and Import under them. Your last choice is remembered in this browser.
  • Import (editors): drop a file anywhere on the tab, or click the drop area to choose one. What it is gets worked out from the file: a backup (.txt), another tool’s CSV (opens the column matching), or overlays (KML, KMZ, GeoJSON, or a shapefile’s files or zip, opening the Map tab to review them). Several files together must all be overlays.
  • Time range: set From and/or To to narrow what gets exported. Leave both blank to include everything.
  • Quick presets (24h / 12h / 6h / 1h): one click fills both pickers relative to now, respecting your active Zulu/Local time mode. Clear, at the top right of the card, empties both to export everything.
  • Filter stats: stacked counts beneath the picker show N of M unique reports + N of M total reports (the second line includes every saved history entry). Turns blue when a filter is active.
  • Backup (.txt): full snapshot with human-readable content at the top and machine-readable JSON at the bottom: the reports with their updates and BDA, the report format, the overlays and the narrative settings. Re-import to merge it back in: a report in both keeps whichever version is newer, and the rest are added. Importing also switches you (only you) to the file’s workspace, sets the tag colors of the operators in it (yours stay as you set them), replaces overlays of the same type and name and takes its report format while yours is the stock one (the prompt says which of these apply), and asks before taking its narrative settings. Option: Export All ATOs bundles every loaded workspace into one file instead of just the active one.
  • Reports for Excel (.csv): flat tabular export. The Identifier column matches the table (Patrol - ALPHA001, following the Title Separator). Current report appears first, then each history entry on its own row labeled Update #N (Old), then each BDA assessment as a BDA #N row (target in Size, effect in Activity, coords in MGRS). Custom builder fields are appended as extra columns after Analysis. Its options: Latest version only, one row per report without its earlier updates (for tools that put every row on a map); and MTI Export, which splits it into two files (_tracked.csv and _wheeled.csv) by scanning Size for vehicle counts written as 4 tracked, 4x tracked, or 4 x wheeled, history entries included.
  • ArcGIS points (Shapefile .zip or .csv): one point per MGRS grid, ready for ArcGIS Pro. Options: Every version and MTI Export. ArcGIS tracks (Shapefile .zip): one line per report that moved. Option: MTI Export. See Overlays and ArcGIS below.
  • CSV Import: maps incoming columns to SALT fields and imports the rows into the workspace you choose: the active one, another loaded workspace, or a staging workspace called IMPORT. From IMPORT, check the rows you want, pick a destination ATO from the dropdown that appears, and send them. A mapped Time column is read as ISO 8601, YYYY-MM-DD HH:MM, HH:MM or HHMM (today, in your time mode; a Z or L suffix overrides it), or a DTG such as 241430Z or 241430ZOCT26. Anything else gets the import time, and the result message says how many rows that happened to.

Workspaces tab

  • Lists every workspace currently loaded with its source (which backup file it was loaded from, or “Uploaded backup”, “Uploaded CSV”, or “In-session”), live report count, and history count.
  • The workspace you are in is highlighted blue and tagged Active. The initials after a workspace’s source are the editors working in it.
  • Each row has an Unload button. The first press asks Unload for everyone? under the workspace's name, and a second unloads it. Unloading clears the workspace from memory and notifies peers; the backup file on disk is preserved, so a server restart would reload it. Undo, for ten seconds after, sends its reports back to the server, each with its own author, and puts you back in it if you were in it; its source then reads Uploaded backup (.txt).
  • Refresh, at the top right, has the server look in its Auto Backups folder again, without a restart: a backup file put there since it started, or changed by hand since it last read or wrote it, is read and its workspaces merged in (a report in both keeps its newer version), and a toast says what came in, such as ALPHA 14 reports new or changed and BRAVO added, and any file that couldn’t be read. It takes only the reports from those files, not their overlays or other settings, so a file dropped in can’t replace the team’s graphics (Recall Settings brings back a finished mission’s). It also lists Previous Missions again, so a mission folder copied into Mission Archives shows up.
  • Done with Mission, at the top right, ends the mission and starts the next from nothing. It asks first, listing what happens, and goes ahead only once you type DONE. Your browser downloads a backup of every workspace (Mission_Title_time.txt, which Import reads). The server moves everything it saved (the backup files, Shared settings.json, audit.log and snapshots/) into a folder of its own in Mission Archives, beside Auto Backups, named for when it finished and its title (2026-10-30 1415Z Patrol): nothing is deleted, and if a file can't be moved (held open by a sync client, say) nothing is reset at all. Then every workspace, overlay, tag and drone, the narrative settings, the report format, FMV mode and the operators' initials are cleared for everyone; the basemaps, the server's certificate and the terrain heights it learned stay. Every browser starts again at the startup box. Its display preferences and initials are put by first, so they can come back. A browser that was offline, or held reports the server never got, offers to save them to a file first: the server won't take them into the new mission. Opened without a server, it backs up and clears this browser only.
  • Previous Missions, under the list once a mission has finished: each one with its workspaces, how many reports, when it finished and who finished it. Recall Settings brings back the parts you tick: overlays, tags, drones and initials are added to what there is now (one of the same name is replaced); the report format, the narrative settings and FMV mode replace what there is. My display preferences and initials brings back this browser's own, as they were before the mission ended. Load brings back the workspaces you tick, for everyone, saved with the new mission; a report in both keeps its newer version. A mission folder copied into Mission Archives by hand is listed too.
  • Activity, at the foot of the tab, in a box four rows tall that scrolls: the last 50 changes made on the server, newest first, such as CD logged ALPHA012 · 2 min ago or AB moved to BRAVO. A change in another workspace than yours says which. It starts empty each time the server starts (Nothing yet since the server started.), and holds no addresses. Viewers see it in their sidebar, under the workspaces.
  • While you are updating a report, a line under its name says when someone else saves it, such as CD updated this report just now. Edit or Update still asks before writing over their change.

Identity tab (person icon)

  • Same identity form as the startup modal: initials, tag color, Set Initials.
  • Shows the current Access Code at the top, hidden as dots until you press Show, with a Copy button that copies it without showing it, for sharing with another editor (display-only: you can’t edit it here; that path is the startup modal).

With sync server: Imports also push the merged result to the server, so peers see the new entries straight away and the server’s backup file is rewritten to include them. Unloads broadcast in real time; one the server refuses (or can’t be reached for) leaves the workspace loaded and says why.

Report Builder

Open the Manage icon (the folder), then the Builder tab. It controls how reports are composed: the order of the form fields and the exact shape of the copied report text. There is one format for every workspace, synced to everyone, so the whole team writes reports the same way. Switching or starting a workspace keeps it; it changes only when someone edits it here. Importing a backup takes the file's format only while yours is still the stock SALT one.

  • A format is an ordered list of lines. Drag a line (or use the arrow buttons) to move it; the ✕ removes it.
  • The Identifier line is special: at the top it renders as the header (like it always has); anywhere lower it renders as a U: line. That is the whole difference between the two presets.
  • SALT preset: identifier on top, then S / A / L / T.
  • SALT-U preset: S / A / L / T first, then U: with the identifier.
  • Analysis is off by default in both presets. Removed built-in lines (including Analysis) appear in the Add back palette; one click restores them. When Analysis is off, its form box and table column hide too.
  • Add Field creates a custom line from two parts: the letter it copies as and what it represents. Example: letter W, name Weather. The form shows a box labeled Weather, the report table gains a Weather column, and the copied text prints W: value. Values save on each report and travel through sync, history, and backups; empty ones are skipped in the copy.
  • Switching presets keeps your custom fields (they re-append after the preset lines).
  • The Live Preview shows the copied-text shape using your latest report (or sample data), updating as you rearrange.
  • Each custom field also gets its own table column, appended after Analysis in format order. The column header shows the descriptive name (hover it to see which letter it copies as). Columns appear, reorder, and disappear the moment you change the format, with no refresh. The built-in columns keep their fixed positions.
  • Removing a custom field only removes its column: the values stay on the reports, so re-adding the field brings them straight back (and they remain in backups and CSV exports either way).
  • Every workspace uses the same format. Add a field on one ATO, switch to another (new or old), and your columns come with you.

Locally: The format is cached per-browser and still applies offline.

With sync server: Format changes broadcast instantly; every editor’s form re-arranges live. The format is stored in every backup file and restored on server restart.

BDA Tracking

Battle Damage Assessment lives on each report but stays completely hidden until you need it: flip Show BDA in the Settings (gear) menu to reveal it (per-browser; flip back to hide).

  • With BDA shown, every report row gets a BDA pill in the Action column. Click it to expand the report’s BDA panel, the same way history expands.
  • The panel is a checklist, not a blank form. It lists the report’s own elements already paired with their grids, one row each. Assess an element with a single click on DEST, DMG, MOB, SUPP, NONE, or ?. A ten-item report is ten clicks, no typing.
  • Pairing: when the number of Size elements matches the number of Location grids they pair in order; when there’s a single grid every element shares it; otherwise the grid is left blank for you to fill. Both fields stay editable on every row.
  • Some of an element: the number box in front of each row starts at all that's left. Change it to log part of it: 3 destroyed of 40x T-72 logs 3x T-72 and leaves 37x T-72 on the checklist for later. What you type or pick in the checklist stays put when another operator's change redraws the table.
  • Picks are staged, not saved. The button reads Log 3 Assessments and commits them all at once as a single sync push. Click a chosen effect again to unstage that row.
  • Assessed elements drop off the checklist, so it always shows only what’s outstanding. When nothing is left it says so.
  • Destroyed comes off the Size. Once vehicles are assessed Destroyed, the report reads what is left: 20x tanks with 3 destroyed shows 17x tanks, with BDA: 3x tanks destroyed small under it (hover it for the Size as logged). Damaged, mobility kill and the other effects still count. Each assessment comes off the element of its type (any case, plural or not, as the checklist pairs them), the rest of the Size stays as written (17x tanks, 2x BMP-2, possible 17x tanks), an element destroyed to none is dropped, and when nothing is left the Size reads None left. An assessment of a type the Size doesn’t name takes nothing off. The Size as logged is kept: editing the report shows it, backups keep it, and deleting, undoing or changing the assessment brings the count back. This holds whether BDA is shown or not.
  • What is left is what the rest of the app reads: copied text says S: 17x tanks (BDA: 3x tanks destroyed); the map’s card, tooltips and table, the Statistics counts, the summaries and narrative, and the CSV, MTI, ArcGIS and Excel exports count what is left. The CSV and the Excel Reports sheet add a Size (as logged) column when BDA has taken from any report, and the ArcGIS layers always carry size_logged. Earlier versions in a report’s history show their Size as it was logged.
  • A roll-up strip above the table totals the whole workspace, counting each vehicle or item (40x T-72 is 40): how many are assessed out of all of them in its reports, then a count per outcome and one for Unassessed. Click an outcome to show only the reports carrying it (Unassessed: reports with elements still to assess); click it again to see everything. Viewers get the strip too, so the read-only port shows the state of the fight at a glance.
  • The pill shows status at a glance: hollow when nothing is assessed, BDA 3/40 while partial, solid once complete, colored by the worst outcome recorded (red destroyed, orange damaged or mobility kill, yellow suppressed, gray no effect or unknown). Hover it for the full count.
  • Assessments can be revised. Click a logged assessment’s effect badge and the effect buttons appear in its place with the current value marked; pick another to change it (Damaged → Destroyed, or back). The revision is re-stamped with the current time and whoever made it, and a toast confirms the change.
  • Each assessment is stamped with the time and your identity, and can be deleted from the same panel: its ✕ asks Delete for everyone? where its time was, a second press deletes it, and Undo puts it back.
  • Assessments sync to every connected browser, append to the copied report text as BDA n: lines, are written into backup files, and appear in CSV exports as BDA #n rows (target in Size, effect in Activity, coords in MGRS).
  • BDA content is searchable: type destroyed or a grid into the search bar to find the reports carrying it.
  • Viewers on the read-only port can see assessments but cannot add or delete them.

Overlays and ArcGIS

Bring your NAIs, OBJs, and phase lines in from ArcGIS, and send your reports back out to it as points for heat maps and analysis.

Importing overlays (Manage → Map tab, editors)

  • In ArcGIS Pro, export the layers as KMZ or KML (Layer To KML), GeoJSON (Features To JSON with Output to GeoJSON and Project to WGS 1984 checked), or a shapefile. Then Import KML / KMZ / GeoJSON / SHP here. Several files at once are fine.
  • Shapefiles: pick the zip ArcGIS makes, or the .shp, .dbf and .prj together. Names come from the attribute table (a NAME field), areas keep their holes. Coordinates in WGS 84 or NAD 83 latitude/longitude, Web Mercator, or a UTM zone are converted; another coordinate system is refused by name, so export it again in WGS 1984.
  • Each shape's type comes from its name (NAI 1, OBJ GOLD, PL BLUE, and PC-12344, or PC with any number, which is an NAI named PC-12344; typed in a Location it names that NAI too), else from its folder, layer, or file name (NAIs, Phase Lines, or the name of one of your tags: a Routes folder puts its graphics in your tag Routes); lines with neither are phase lines. A line named AXIS GOLD, AOA 2 or Axis of advance North (or in a folder or layer called Axes of advance, Axis or AOA) is an axis of advance. An area or point with none of these is Unknown: it is added with no tag, to be sorted later (below). Review the list, set or change the type of any shape (or Skip it), then Add.
  • Sort while importing: check shapes in the review (Shift-click a second checkbox to check every one between), then in the bar above the list pick what they are under Use as (NAI, OBJ, PL, Axis of advance, one of your tags, Unknown, or Skip) and Apply. They stay checked, so you can pick again; Clear lets them go. Each shape's own Use as is still beside it.
  • Areas are NAIs and OBJs; lines are phase lines or axes of advance; points are kept as labels only. An NAI or OBJ drawn as a path (Google Earth's Add Path) is closed back to its start and used as an area. Importing a shape with the same type and name again replaces it, so an updated overlay file just replaces the old one.
  • Overlays are one set for every workspace: import them once and every ATO, editor and viewer uses them. They are saved in every workspace's backup file. The set holds up to 2,000 graphics.
  • The tab groups them as NAIs, Objectives and Phase lines, each with how many reports in the workspace on screen name it. Each group folds, closed to start with (Unknown, open): click its header to open it, or the two buttons at the right of Import to open or close them all. Unload on a group removes that group, and Unload All removes them all: each asks on its own face first (Unload NAIs for everyone?, Unload all for everyone?), and a second press unloads. The × removes one for everyone: the first press asks Remove for everyone? in its name, and a second removes it. Any other button lets a question go, and Undo puts back what went, beside anything imported since. When one changes, import the new file: it replaces the old one of the same type and name. Reports themselves are never changed by this.

Tags: NAI, OBJ, PL, Axis of advance and your own (Manage → Map tab, editors)

  • Every graphic has one tag. NAI, OBJ, PL and Axis of advance are built in; under Tags you add your own for anything else (routes, engagement areas, checkpoints): + New tag, type a name, pick a color, and Add Tag (or Enter; Escape or Cancel closes it). The box is one row however many tags you have: pick a tag in its list (which says how many there are) to see its color, name and how many graphics have it. Change its name in its box (Enter, or click away; Escape puts it back) or its color with its swatch. Its × asks Delete for everyone? in its name, and a second press deletes it: its graphics stay, Unknown, and Undo gives it back to them. Tags are shared with every editor and kept in every backup file; at most 30.
  • Axis of advance (AXIS): a line the enemy is moving along, drawn from where they come from to where they head. It is drawn in dark red on a white edge with an arrowhead at its end, and has a switch in the map's key while there is one. It names no place: clicking it on the map opens its card instead (see The Map), and the Narrative reads the enemy's way of march from it (see Narrative settings, Enemy moves toward). A line drawn the wrong way round is imported again the other way, or drawn so in ArcGIS.
  • Each graphic's tag is in the list beside it: pick NAI, OBJ, PL, Axis of advance, one of yours, or Unknown. A shape the import didn't recognize (its name doesn't say NAI, OBJ or PL) is one pick from being one. Moved into NAI, OBJ or PL, it is named as an import names it, from its name in the file: NAI 3 made an OBJ is OBJ 3, and Bridge made an OBJ is OBJ BRIDGE. Moved into one of your tags, it keeps its name in the file.
  • Several at once: check them (Shift-click a second checkbox to check every one shown between), then in the bar above the lists pick a tag and Add to Tag, or Remove from Tag to leave them with none (Unknown). Undo gives them back the tags they had. One that would have the same name as a graphic already in that tag is left as it is, and the message says so.
  • The lists are grouped by tag: Unknown first (the graphics with no tag, open, with how many), then NAIs, Objectives, Phase lines, Axes of advance, then yours. Unknown graphics aren't on the map (nor in its key, a saved map or a print) and name nothing until you give them a tag. An import's Use as list has your tags too.
  • In reports: a report logged inside an area of one of your tags gets that area's name in its Location, as with an NAI (38SMB4200092000, Bridge); its name typed in the Location counts too. Lines and points of your tags are drawn but don't name reports. The Statistics, the Summary Reports and the Narrative count NAIs, OBJs and phase lines only; the Narrative reads axes of advance for the way the enemy moves.

How the Location box is read

  • Keep typing it the usual way, for example 38S MB 12345 67890, NAI 1. The grid and the place are both picked out. Spacing and case don't matter, and NAI 01, NAI1, and nai 1 are all NAI 1. Several grids in one box are several points.
  • Named for you. While you type, blue chips under the Location box show the NAI or OBJ the grid is in, any area of your own tags it is in, and a phase line within 1 km (with its distance). When you Log or Update, they are added to the Location, for example 11S NV 3921 0547, NAI 2, PL BLUE. Click a chip to add it at once. Nothing is added for a type you typed yourself.
  • Typed one disagrees with the grid? An orange chip says so while you type, and Log asks you to settle it: use the grid's (Use NAI 2, written where you typed NAI 1), keep yours, or keep both. Cancel goes back to the box. When the grid is in none, you can keep yours or take it out. The same goes for a typed phase line more than 1 km from the grid.
  • Older reports that disagree carry an orange Check tag under the Location. Click it to settle it the same way; the change is saved as an Update, so the old Location stays in the history.
  • Without a typed name, a report is still placed by its grid: a small tag shows the NAI, OBJ, or phase line (hover for the distance).

Activity tags

  • The Activity text is tagged Moving, Stationary, Dismounted, Emplacing, Firing, Observing, or Resupplying from the words in it (a report can carry several), plus a heading when one is given: moving N, heading 270, NB, westbound.

Export for ArcGIS (Import / Export tab)

  • Shapefile (.zip), the easiest: unzip it, then drag the .shp onto the map in ArcGIS Pro, or browse to it from a folder connection; ArcGIS Online takes the zip as it is. In WGS 1984, text in UTF-8; field names are cut to the format's 10 characters (head_deg, prec_m) and text to 254.
  • .csv, every character of text: run XY Table To Point with lon as X and lat as Y (WGS 1984). Both hold the same rows; the time filter applies.
  • One point per grid, at the center of the square the grid names. Each carries the report's identifier, time, Size, Activity, its tags and heading (heading_deg, for rotating arrow symbols), the MGRS, the NAI and OBJ (with nai_from / obj_from: typed or grid), the nearest phase line and its distance in meters, any loc_check, the BDA, and custom fields. Reports with no readable grid are left out, and the message says how many.
  • Heat map: select the new layer, open Symbology, and choose Heat Map. For analysis, Kernel Density or Optimized Hot Spot Analysis run on the same layer.
  • ArcGIS tracks (Shapefile .zip): one line per report that moved, from its first grid through its updates to its latest, in the order reported. Each carries its identifier, Size, Activity, start and end times, the number of positions, the length in meters, and the direction (bearing_deg, direction). In ArcGIS Pro, give the layer a line symbol with an arrow at its end to show the way it went. A report that never moved is not a line; it is in the points.
  • The points hold each report once (per grid it names), as it is now: its earlier updates are not extra points, so a heat map counts each formation once. For a CSV to load into another tool that plots every row, tick Latest version only on Reports for Excel (.csv).
  • Every version (points): one point for each saved version of a report, its updates included, at that version's grids and with its own Size, Activity, time and reporter, oldest first; version numbers them (2/3) and latest says which is the report as it is now. The file ends in _versions.
  • MTI Export (points and tracks): two files, _tracked and _wheeled, read from Size as the CSV's MTI Export reads it. In each, Size is the count (4x tracked) and vehicles its number, for sizing symbols; a report with both is in both. A file with nothing in it isn't saved.

Locally: Overlays are kept in this browser, for every workspace in it, and work the same way.

With sync server: Overlays sync to every editor and viewer and are restored on server restart. Viewers can use them on the map and export for ArcGIS, but not change them.

The Map

Under the Report Log, one button away: your overlays and reports on a map, with a table of what is on it. Viewers get it too.

  • The map button beside the Report Log heading slides down to the map; the same button, a list by then, slides back. The Report Log's bar stays above both, and its search narrows the map, its numbers and its table as well. Beside the map is what's on it, and under it the table of those reports. Everything covers the workspace on screen.
  • The overlays drawn on a map: NAIs green, OBJs blue, phase lines black (with a white edge, so they show on any background), and axes of advance dark red with an arrowhead where the enemy heads. The tags under each Location use the same colors. Your own tags are drawn in their colors: areas filled and outlined, lines in the color on a black edge, points as diamonds. Unknown graphics, with no tag, aren't drawn until they have one.
  • The key is a switch for each tag (NAI, OBJ, PL and each of yours with graphics): click one to hide its graphics, and again to show them. One switched off is dimmed, can't be clicked on the map, and is left out of a saved or printed map's key. Kept in this browser.
  • Layers, each turned on and off by itself, so they can be seen together: the Heat map (on to start with), where the reports are thickest, blue through green and yellow to red at the busiest; Tracks, each report's path from its first position through its updates to its latest, with an arrow for the way it went (a report at one grid is a dot, with a short arrow when its Activity gives a heading); and Reports, each report as a dot. Hover a track or a dot for its report, distance, direction and times. Your layers are kept in this browser.
  • Latest Grids or All Reports, for the heat map and the dots: each report once, at its latest grid (a report with several grids is shared between them); or every saved report, its updates included, at its grids, the earlier dots smaller.
  • Click a report (a dot or a track) for its card: what it says, its time, BDA and updates. Show in Table finds its row; Edit (editors) puts it in the form to update. Its ring stays on the map until you pick another or click an empty spot. A picked track shows the time at each of its positions.
  • Sight and reach: a picked report that observes (reconnaissance, air defense, a command post or a UAV team halted or observing, or anyone observing) or fires direct from a position draws its lines of sight to the NAIs and OBJs near it, as the Narrative reads them: to each one’s nearest edge and its middle, green where it sees, green to the ground that hides it, an X there, and red past it; gray and dashed while the heights are still coming, or with no elevation source. Each kind of reach its weapons have is a dashed ring with the type and its range (orange direct fire, purple indirect fire, light blue air defense). Its card says the same in words, under Sight and Reach.
  • Rate of march: a report whose track has times says on its card how fast it went: overall (from leaving its first grid to reaching its latest, halts included), with the distance and time, then each leg’s. It is reckoned for what the report is, from the equipment catalogue: aircraft (km/h or knots), dismounts when it is people on foot (km/h or mph), otherwise vehicles (km/h or mph). A leg with no time between its updates, a time that can’t be read, or a speed nothing of its kind could make (a typo, or a late report) is left out rather than shown as a silly number. Opening a report’s updates in the Report Log shows the same under each time: the rate from the grid before, and overall on the report itself.
  • Click a row in the table and the map moves to that report (keeping the zoom), rings it, and opens its card.
  • Click an NAI, OBJ or phase line (or a graphic of your own tags) to narrow the table and the numbers to it; it's drawn bolder, and a chip above the table says which. Click it again, or the chip, to show every place. The Busiest places in the sidebar do the same.
  • Click an axis of advance for its card: its name and length, drawn wider while the card is open. Editors have Remove on it: the first press asks Remove for everyone? on the button, a second removes it for everyone, and Undo puts it back. Esc, the × or a click on an empty spot closes the card.
  • Only what's on the map (above the table, on to start with): the table and the numbers hold only the reports in the part of the map on screen, so zooming and panning narrow them. Untick it for every report in the window.
  • Above the map: how many reports are on it, how many are in an NAI or OBJ, and BDA assessed. In the sidebar left of it: every activity with how many, and the busiest places; the sidebar button at the top left of the map slides it away and back (the map keeps its size). Click an activity to show only reports with it, on the map, in the numbers and in the table; click more to add them, or Show All.
  • Window: All, or the last 24, 12, or 6 hours. The key and, under it, Up to sit inside the map at its foot: the slider moves the window's end back from now to the first report, to see how things stood then; Now brings it back.
  • Play, beside Up to, walks the slider from the first report to now in about ten seconds, the map following, so movement can be watched; Pause stops it where it is, and moving the slider or Now ends it.
  • Ctrl + scroll to zoom (⌘ + scroll on a Mac, or pinch on a trackpad; in full screen a plain scroll zooms), drag to move (the picture slides with the pointer and is drawn afresh when you let go), double-click to zoom in; +, − and the four-arrows button (Show all). The map covers your overlays and reports and can't be zoomed out past them. Names stay on screen as you zoom in: a shape's name moves to the part of it in view. A plain scroll over the map scrolls the page on, down to the table.
  • Names never overlap. Where two would, the one that matters more is shown: first what you are measuring, then what you picked (a report's track and times, a place, an axis, a grid gone to), then axes of advance and phase lines, then the places with reports (the most first), then the rest (the biggest first), and last the tracks' and drones' names. A graphic's name shows only when the graphic is big enough on screen to hold it, so with hundreds of NAIs zoomed out the map shows their shapes, and their names come as you zoom in.
  • Go to a grid: the target button under Show all opens a small box. Type or paste an MGRS grid (with MGRS Lock on, its digits alone take the lock's zone and square) and press Enter or Go: a yellow X marks it, with its grid beside it, and the map moves to show it; Zoom to it zooms in on it. A grid that can't be read is said in the box. Closing the box (its ×, Esc, or the button again) takes the X away. It is yours alone, and not kept.
  • Full screen: the corners button under the target button fills the screen with the map, its controls and key; Esc or the button again leaves.
  • The layers button under the map's zoom buttons switches the basemap: pick one, or No basemap. Editors add and change basemaps in Manage → Map; the button's menu has a link there.
  • FMV mode (Settings menu, FMV Mode: On / Off, for the whole team): on, the map has its drone button, the drones and their views, and the report form has an Observer; off, none of them, and the reports keep the observers they have. Until someone sets it, it is on when the team has drones (so a team already using them sees no change) and off when it has none.
  • Drones: the drone button under the layers button opens a small menu. On the map draws each drone on station, in orange with its name; Their view draws a cone from each drone to where it’s looking: the grid it’s been given to look at, else its own newest report on the map (one logged with it as the Observer), and with neither, no view. So several drones each look at what they saw, not all at the one newest report. The cone is narrow at the drone and widens to a smooth, rounded end round the report, with the sightline down its middle, a box round the report and the distance and direction. It points at what the drone is watching; it isn’t the camera’s footprint. It follows the time slider and the filters. Both switches are kept in your browser.
  • Each drone is one row: its station switch (off station, it stays in the list, dimmed, but leaves the map and the Observer list), its name (click it to zoom the map in on it), its grid, and ··· (More), which opens Looking at (a grid for its view to point at; press Enter, or empty it to go back to its newest report), Range (how far it can see, in km: a faint ring round the drone shows it, and a target past it turns the cone and sightline red, with how far out of range it is beside them) and Remove (the first press asks Remove for everyone? on the button, a second removes it, and Undo puts it back where it was). Editors add a drone with +: a name (e.g. GE 1) and its grid. They move one by typing a new grid in its row and pressing Enter (digits alone take MGRS Lock’s zone and square), and switch it on or off station. The drones are shared with everyone; hover one for when it was put there, where it’s looking and its range.
  • Each drone’s name on the map says how long ago it was put there (GE 1 · moved 6h ago), so a position left for hours shows it. Report dots have a white center, so they stay visible on the red of a busy heat map.
  • Grab a grid: hold Shift over the map and the grid under the pointer shows beside it; Shift + click copies that grid, ten digits, to paste anywhere. A Shift + click on a report copies its grid instead of opening its card.
  • Measure: the ruler button. Pressing it slides its tools out beside it, with Distance picked; pick another there. What each measures shows on the map and, with its grids, at the foot of the map. Esc or the ruler again puts the tools away. While measuring, a click measures and opens no report, and a drag still moves the map (except to drag out a range ring). Distances read in your Units (Settings); bearings in degrees with the compass point.
    • Distance: click a point, then another: a white line joins them, with the distance and bearing beside it (for example 2.40 km, 047° NE). A third click starts a new line from there.
    • Path: click points along the way; each leg shows its distance and bearing, and the end the total. Double-click or press Enter to finish; the next click starts another.
    • Area: click round a shape, three points or more: its area and perimeter. Double-click or Enter finishes it.
    • Range ring: click the center, then the radius (or press at the center and drag out to it): the ring with its radius. Keep ring on the map leaves it there after measuring, in this browser only; the kept rings are listed under the tools while Range ring is picked, each with × to remove it, and Clear all.
    • Time-distance: type a speed and pick its unit beside it (km/h or m/min with Settings > Units on Metric; mph or knots on Imperial, knots giving the distance in nautical miles), then click along the route: its length and how long it takes. The box is empty the first time; after that this browser keeps the last speed and unit. Double-click or Enter finishes it.
    • Line of sight: click where the eye is, then what it looks at: whether it can be seen over the ground (the eye and the target 2 m up, the earth’s curve allowed for), and if not, where the ground hides it and by how much; the line is drawn as a picked report’s are. It needs an elevation source (Narrative settings, Terrain), and checks lines up to 20 km. The heights along the line are asked for once and kept for everyone. Where the source has no height at either end (water, or past its coverage) it says so; when heights aren’t coming (the source failing, or the page opened as a file) it says why, as a picked report’s card does.
    • Draw axis (editors): click from where the enemy comes from, along its way, to where it heads; double-click or Enter finishes it. Name it (the first free number is offered: AXIS 1) and Save axis (or Enter): it is an axis of advance for everyone, drawn with its arrowhead and read by the Narrative like one imported, with Undo for a moment after. A name in use asks for another.
    • From the keyboard: Tab to the ruler and its tools; with the map itself focused, the arrow keys move it under the crosshair in its middle, Space puts a point there, and Enter finishes.
  • Save or print: the arrow button under the layers button. Save as PNG saves the view as it is on screen, with a title (the workspace, its window and when it was made), a north arrow, a scale bar (in the units of Settings > Units) and the key. The basemap goes in when its service lets the page copy its images; when it doesn’t, the image says it was left out, as it does for a basemap image that hasn’t come after 8 seconds. Print lays out the same on a landscape page, basemap included, and the print dialog can also save it as a PDF.
  • Basemaps (editors, in Manage → Map → Basemaps, the second of the tab’s two, beside Graphics): one box for both kinds of service, and Add; each is named for itself (a WMS by its title, a tile service by its host). Paste the link you use in other tools:
    • A WMS (for ArcGIS, the address ending in WMSServer): its layers are looked up and listed (with a filter when there are many); tick the ones you want and Add puts them in as one basemap, named after the service. It asks the service in its own terms: its WMS version, its image format, and Web Mercator when it offers it, otherwise latitude / longitude, stretched to fit. A layer it can't show (only in some other projection) says so.
    • A tile address (WMTS, ArcGIS, or XYZ, in Web Mercator): anything with {z}, {x}, {y}, or {TileMatrix}, {TileRow}, {TileCol} is added as it is.
  • Many services won't let a web page read their layer list; then the sync server reads it for you (the list says read by the server). If neither can reach the service, the message says so, and you can still type layer names to try.
  • Check beside a service asks it for a little of the map and says what came back: a map image (the message goes after a few seconds), or the service's own error (a wrong layer name, a projection it doesn't offer, a login it wants), which stays until you check again. The list is shared with everyone.
  • Layers beside a WMS opens it again: its layers are looked up afresh with the ones in use ticked (one the service no longer lists says not listed); tick or untick, and Save. Edit beside a tile service changes its address. Remove, at the end of each row, takes the service out for everyone: the first press asks Remove for everyone? in the service's name, and a second press removes it (any other button lets it go). Undo puts it back where it was.
  • The picker behind the layers button switches between them, or to No basemap; each person's pick is kept in their browser. Only the area of the map is requested, never the whole world. Each browser fetches it straight from the service, so a computer that can't reach a service shows the map without it, and says so in a note under the map's controls (editors: Check, in Manage → Map, says why).
  • Viewers have the map too, with everything but Edit, adding basemaps and moving drones (the same basemaps and drones as the editors; they can switch and zoom, and grab grids).

Locally: The basemaps are kept in this browser, and the map works the same way.

With sync server: The basemaps and the drones sync to every editor and viewer and are restored on server restart; each browser picks which basemap it shows, and whether the drones and their view show. Your layers, Latest grids / All reports, the sidebar and Only what's on the map are kept in your browser.

Statistics

Open with the Statistics icon (the bar chart). It follows the log live. Viewers get it too.

  • Scope: This ATO (the workspace on screen) or All ATOs (every loaded workspace together). All ATOs adds a Reports per ATO chart, and its exports are named All_ATOs. Remembered in this browser.
  • Window: All, the last 7 days, 24, 12, or 6 hours, or your own From / To (date and time, in your Zulu or Local mode); Clear above the To time goes back to all. Every number and chart covers the same window.
  • Headline numbers: reports, how many have a grid, how many are in an NAI or OBJ, how many have a Check to look at, and BDA assessed (vehicles and items).
  • Charts: reports per NAI, per OBJ, and at each phase line (typed, or a grid within 1 km of the line); reports by hour of day and by day; activity tags; the heading of moving reports; what was reported by type, counted from Size (2x T-72, 4 BMP-2); and BDA outcomes. Each report counts once per place or tag it names; history is not counted. Long lists show their top ten and an Other bar.
  • Hover a bar (or Tab to it) for its number. Table shows every value under the chart.
  • PNG, SVG, and CSV on each chart export it, on a light background for slides and print, with its title, workspace, and window written in. Charts (.zip) saves every chart as PNG and SVG, and all the numbers in one CSV, together in one zip file.
  • Export to Excel saves one workbook (.xlsx). The Statistics sheet has the headline numbers, then each chart’s table with an Excel chart drawn from it beside it: change a number and the chart follows; restyle it like any Excel chart. A bar chart shows the top ten; its table has every value. The Reports sheet is the report table for the same window, one row per report as it is now, with what the app reads from it (NAI / OBJ / PL, activity tags, heading, BDA, how many updates) and times as real dates, as an Excel table you can sort and filter. Every table in the workbook (the headline numbers, each chart's table, the reports) has thin outlines on all its cells, header included.

Summary Reports

Open with the Summary Reports icon (the page). Pick a report and a window, and it is written for you from the reports you already have. It updates live, and viewers can use it too.

  • Rollup: totals and the change from the period before; each NAI, OBJ, and phase line with its count and activity; activity overall; what was reported by type; notable reports (firing or emplacing); BDA; and anything to check.
  • Most Trafficked: the busiest NAIs and OBJs, phase lines, and 1 km grid squares, each with its count, peak hour, and most common activity, then the busiest hours. This is the written heat map.
  • Place Activity: pick an NAI, OBJ, or phase line, and get every report there in time order, with a count, its activity, and its busiest hour.
  • Shift Brief: the short handover. Report count against the previous shift, the most active places, activity, the last report, open items (checks and unassessed BDA), and the last five reports.
  • Narrative: the period in plain sentences, the bottom line first.
    • BLUF (bottom line up front): the strongest judgment, and beside it the strongest of another kind when it is at least likely and no more than a step weaker (a defense in one place while they attack in another), each with its letter in the assessment and its confidence; then Strength, the largest single sighting and the unit size it suggests (armor and mechanized first), with the combat support seen (recon, artillery, air defense, engineers, EW and so on); Mobility, how many tracked and wheeled vehicles and what that means (mostly tracked: can move across country; mostly wheeled: faster on roads, and tied to them more); Closest, the objective a report is nearest, how far, and whether it is closing on it; Threat, the three NAIs or OBJs most under the enemy’s weapons, with how many reports cover each by direct fire, reach it by indirect fire and cover the air over it (Reach, below); Terrain, with an elevation source (Narrative settings, Terrain), the heights the reports are at, how many are on high ground, columns on slopes too steep for them, artillery behind a crest, and how many observers can see into which NAIs and OBJs; Activity, how many reports from how many observers and, over a window, up or down from the period before, the busiest places and the tempo; Latest, the newest report; Watch for, the first three signs that would firm up a judgment; and Gap, the first gap below.
    • 1. Situation and 2. Enemy forces: what the reports say, in short items. How many and from how many observers; the tempo, reports in the last 3 hours against the average 3 hours before (rising, steady or falling; none at all may be a gap in coverage); where things headed and the way of march; phase lines crossed, from a report’s track between its updates, and reported at; each objective with the report nearest it, how far, and whether its track closed on it (km/h) or drew away; what is new this period (Russian or Chinese types, and places, that the period before had none of); the latest firing; and latest by place, each busy place as last seen, with how what was there moves and, with an elevation source, the ground's height there and its steepest slope. Then the equipment reported, by kind (armor, mechanized infantry, reconnaissance, artillery, air defense, engineers, logistics and support, and so on); mobility, how it all moves (tracked, wheeled, towed, carried, on foot, in the air), from the catalogue; columns, reports of four or more vehicles moving, with their mix and rate of march (from the report’s track), checked against how they move: road-march speed (20 km/h or more for mostly tracked, 30 for mostly wheeled, 25 for a mix), faster than tracked vehicles go (over 65 km/h: check the type or the times), or slow for an hour or more (under 5 km/h: halts, or across country), and with an elevation source on a slope too steep for them (over 30% for mostly wheeled, 60% for mostly tracked: check the type or the grid); Defense: preparing a defense, high ground held (observers, air defense, radars or command vehicles halted on high ground within 3 km of an NAI or OBJ, a step firmer when they can see into it; with an elevation source only), a company strongpoint, a fortified line in depth, a security zone or screen, antitank positions, remote mining, camouflage and deception (maskirovka). Fires: an artillery preparation or fire strike, UAVs spotting for artillery (a reconnaissance-fire complex), persistent drone surveillance, counter-battery fire, shoot and scoot, tanks as fire support, thermobaric fires (TOS, Shmel), long-range strikes, strikes on command and sensors, suppressing air defense, loitering munitions and drones, drones cutting supply routes, glide bombs, attack aviation. Support: a river or gap crossing, breaching, chemical-defense and smoke troops, a command post, air defense over a key site, electronic warfare, a logistics point, logistics pushing forward, a railhead, resupply by drone, a helicopter refueling point, casualty evacuation, regrouping after an attack. Vehicles given only as tracked or wheeled, as MTI reports give them (10x wheeled vics, 2x tracked), are added up under one name each in Enemy forces, with a line for their mix. Mostly tracked (two thirds or more) reads as armor or mechanized forces, the wheeled as their support; mostly wheeled, as a supply or motorized column, the tracked as its escort; otherwise, a mechanized force with its supply vehicles. The other rules read them that way too, as they read a probable type: a sign seen only in such reports is a step weaker. Their own rule says what the force is, its approximate size from the tracked vehicles, and whether it moved or halted; it is roughly even chance from one report, likely with 10 vehicles or more in one report or a second report like it in the same place within 6 hrs, and never more than likely, since the type is still to confirm.
      Each judgment uses the doctrine of the army whose equipment is in its reports (else the period's): a Doctrine line says what that army typically does, and the judgment uses its terms where they differ (a Russian forward detachment, a PLA vanguard; DRG or special-operations teams; a reconnaissance-fire complex or reconnaissance-strike). The ten strongest are shown, and how many more there were. Each judgment says how likely it is in the intelligence community's terms (ICD 203: almost no chance, very unlikely, unlikely, roughly even chance, likely, very likely, almost certain), lists the reports behind it, gives its confidence (high, moderate, or low, from how many reports and observers support it) in a sentence of its own, and what to watch for that would make it firmer. Rules never go past very likely.
      On screen, Details at the end of a judgment’s line opens its basis, doctrine, unit and confidence (Hide closes it), and From under them lists the reports behind it by number (the first eight, then and N more, which shows the rest). Tap one to see it in the Report Log: its row is brought into view and flashes, and a search or BDA filter that hides it is cleared. The lists are on screen only: Copy, Save .txt and Print have the text alone, and the lists are hidden while you edit the text and under your own wording.

Places with no reports for 6 hours or more (in a window of 12 hours or more; without a window, over the reports’ whole span), quietest first, to re-task collection to; judgments at likely or more resting on low confidence; reports without a grid; equipment not in the catalogue, to add under the settings’ Equipment; the terrain: no elevation source, lines of sight not known yet (with why, when the source is failing) or not known (no height from the source at an end of the line), or reports whose height hasn't come in yet (and why the source last failed); and types that fight whose reach the catalogue doesn't give, to give under Equipment (ballistic and cruise missiles and drones aside: they reach past the areas). Neither the terrain nor reach is ever the bottom line’s gap.

Judgment detail: each judgment in full, as before: its likelihood with its percentage, its basis citing the reports, its doctrine, the unit from the unit list, its confidence and what to watch for. On screen it is one line, as each judgment opens under its own Details; Copy, Save .txt and Print have it in full.

A vic, vehicle, tracked or wheeled with nothing more said is counted as other vehicles; the more specific word wins (engineer vic is engineers, wheeled APC an APC). A variant written after a hyphen and a digit (Kornet-D 9P163-3) is listed too. How sure the observer was, before the type or the count (2x possible tanks, 3x poss APCs, 1x probable heli, 2x likely a heli, susp 2x Mi-8), is read apart from the type: the equipment is counted as usual, the reports keep the word, and in the Narrative a sign seen only in such reports still lets a rule fire but adds no strength (a step off its level for each, down to roughly even chance), and its reports are cited as possible T1 250100Z. A type it doesn't know is listed under Gaps.
    Sizes are read the way they're written: 2x T-72 and 4 BMP-2, vics 10x, approx 6 trucks. Brackets that aren't a breakdown (3x T-72 (one damaged)) are a note.
  • Narrative settings, in a column of their own to the right of the Narrative, the full height of the view; the report updates as you change them:

    • Enemy moves toward: the enemy's way of march in the AO, a compass point (N, NE, E, SE, S, SW, W, NW), or None (the narrative as without it). Situation says the way of march and how many moving reports went each way. In the Assessment: armor or other combat forces moving back along it are withdrawing, as if the report said so (a withdrawal judgment, and not counted as advancing); moving along it is a sign for preparing an offensive and for logistics pushing forward, and makes a forward detachment, an advance on several axes, an exploitation or a pursuit a step firmer; moving across it makes an envelopment a step firmer. The Basis names the sign with the way of march, e.g. moving along the way of march (W), and one not yet seen is a Watch for.
    • Units: each unit's name; Where it operates, a dropdown of the NAIs and OBJs (pick several; none: anywhere), with Other for places not in it, like a 10 km square 11S NV 31, typed in the box it brings up; and What it has, a dropdown of kinds of equipment (none: any).     • Equipment: types in your reports that the catalogue doesn't know come first (a dot on the tab says there are some): tap one, pick what it is, Save. Moves on says how it moves: tracked, wheeled, towed, carried, on foot, flies or fixed (or not said, when it is made both ways). Reach is how far its main weapon reaches, in km, for armor, mechanized infantry, antitank weapons, artillery and air defense (empty keeps the catalogue’s, if it has one). Look up a type says what the catalogue makes of any name, how it moves and how far it reaches; add your own, or put a built-in one right for your team (yours win).
    • Rules: your own come first, with + Add a rule at the right of their heading; under them, switch any built-in rule on or off. Each of your own says what it judges (“are preparing a river crossing”), the words to look for (“bridge, ferry, PMP”), and how many reports within how many hours in one place. Yours start at roughly even chance; one more report makes them likely, three more very likely.
    • Tap a unit, entry or rule to change it; Remove asks once first (Remove for everyone?), and Undo puts it back.
    • Terrain: where the ground's heights come from, for the narrative to read slopes and high ground. Pick a saved basemap that holds heights: a WMS layer that answers what is the height here with a number (GetFeatureInfo; a DEM layer, such as GeoServer's or ArcGIS's), with its layer if it has several; or elevation tiles, in Terrarium or Mapbox Terrain-RGB encoding. Say whether its heights are in meters or feet. Test as elevation asks it the height at a grid you type, and says what came back: the height, or why there is none (the service can't be reached, its reply holds no number, and the start of that reply). Those can be many points: on a slow service the first time takes a while, and after that they are kept. Heights still being asked come in a moment later.

Sync and Server Status Online only

This section only applies when the Python sync server is running. Without the server, the tool operates entirely from your browser’s local storage.

  • The status dot next to “Report Log”: green = connected and synced, red = locally run with no server connection (working from local storage only).
  • Connected users count shows the number of unique people on the server. Multiple tabs open on the same machine count once.
  • Changes made while locally run queue in your browser and replay automatically the moment the server comes back.
  • Each change sends only the reports it touched, not the whole workspace, so syncing stays quick however long the operation runs.
  • If the server refuses a report (for example a field longer than it accepts), a red message gives the reason and the report stays in your table marked Not saved; hover the mark for the reason. Fix it (Edit or Update) and it is sent again; or click Discard (or delete it) to drop the change and get the server's copy back. The rest of your reports keep syncing either way.
  • A red Not saving bar at the top means your changes have waited more than 30 seconds for the server. They are kept in this browser and sent when it can be reached; closing the tab while they wait asks first. A bar saying This page is version X and the server is Y means the server was upgraded since this page was opened: press Reload. The version this page is shows small at the foot of the right-hand panel, centered, in the same place on every view.
  • Anything that goes wrong unexpectedly shows as a red message beginning Something went wrong. Your reports are kept; reload the page if it misbehaves.
  • Two reports can end up with one number when two editors log at once, or one logs while the server can’t be reached. Both rows are marked Duplicate in orange; Renumber on either gives that report the next free number in its workspace (saved as an Edit: no update entry).
  • Anything else the server doesn’t take (the workspace, your identity, the overlays, the report format, an unload) says so and why: Not saved, or Not unloaded. Changes made while it can’t be reached say they’re saved here; reports are sent, workspace by workspace, as soon as it’s back. The workspace you set and your identity aren’t sent later, so they say Saved here only.

What the Sync Server Does Automatically

  • Auto-opens your browser to the tool when you launch the server.
  • Auto-minimizes the console window so it stays out of the way while running in the background.
  • Creates the Auto Backups folder next to itself on first run if it doesn’t already exist.
  • Auto-loads all backup files on startup: it scans the Auto Backups folder for {ATO}_{Title}.txt files and loads every workspace it finds, so your data is always ready without any manual import step.
  • Auto-saves after every change: any log, edit, update, or delete immediately rewrites that workspace’s own backup file, so nothing is lost if the server restarts. Each workspace has its own file; unloading one leaves its file in place.
  • Keeps an audit log, Auto Backups/audit.log: one line per change with the time, who made it (initials and address), and what it was, plus server starts and stops. Nothing in the app reads it; it is there for the question “who changed this, and when?”
  • Archives finished missions in Mission Archives, beside Auto Backups, one folder each, when an editor presses Done with Mission (Manage > Workspaces).
  • Keeps dated snapshots in Auto Backups/snapshots/: one when the server starts, then at most one an hour, the newest 48 kept. If something goes badly wrong (a bad import, a wrong delete), stop the server, copy the snapshot you want over the live backup file, and start it again.
  • Broadcasts changes to all clients in real time via Server-Sent Events, so every connected browser updates instantly without anyone needing to refresh.
  • Generates a fresh access code on every launch (shown in the console) so a previous session’s code can’t be reused.
  • Encrypts every connection (HTTPS), so nobody watching the network can read the reports or the access code, or copy the code to get in. On first start it makes its own certificate, kept in Auto Backups/tls/. Old http:// addresses are sent on to https://. --no-https turns this off; --cert and --key use a certificate of your own.
  • Validates every request: malformed payloads are rejected with a clear error, a rate limiter throttles abuse per-IP, and edits to existing reports are server-stamped with the operator the server knows is behind that connection (so attribution can’t be spoofed by a misbehaving client).
  • Atomic disk writes: backup files are written via a temp-file-then-rename, so a crash mid-save can never leave a corrupted backup.
  • Graceful drain on Ctrl+C: notifies connected clients, flushes any pending disk write, then exits.

Boot Dashboard

On launch the server prints a compact boot banner naming itself, then these labeled lines:

  • Workbench: the LAN URL editors hit to use the tool (default port 8080).
  • Access Code: the 10-character per-launch code editors must enter on the startup modal. Shows UNGATED when started with --no-access-code.
  • Read Only: the read-only viewer URL (default port 8081). Omitted when --viewer-port 0 disables the listener.
  • Certificate: the SHA-256 fingerprint of the server’s certificate, on two lines. Shows Plain HTTP instead when started with --no-https.

The Browser Warning Online only

The server makes its own certificate, and no authority vouches for it, so each browser warns once that the connection isn’t private. Choose Advanced, then Proceed (Firefox: Accept the Risk and Continue).

  • To be sure it’s really your server and not a look-alike, open the certificate details from the warning (or the padlock afterwards) and compare its SHA-256 fingerprint with the Certificate lines in the server console.
  • The browser asks again if the server’s address changes, since the certificate is made again for the new address.
  • Basemaps on an http:// address still show: the server fetches their map images for the page, from the hosts of saved basemaps only.

Access Code Online only

The editor port is gated by a 10-character code that the server generates on every launch. The host shares it once; editors enter it once per server session.

  • The code uses only characters that are unambiguous to share verbally over a radio (no 0/O, no 1/I/l, no 5/S).
  • The host’s own browser auto-fills the code on the startup modal. They review identity/ATO/title and click Continue.
  • The code is hidden as dots wherever the page shows it, so nobody can read it over your shoulder. Show beside it reveals it until you leave that screen.
  • Other editors see the modal with an empty Access Code field. They type what the host shared with them; the server validates on submit and rejects with a red inline error if wrong.
  • Once entered correctly, the code is cached per-browser; refreshing the page doesn’t re-prompt. Restarting the server rotates the code, so on the next visit the modal reappears for re-confirmation.
  • Local mode (opening the HTML file directly, no server): the access-code field is hidden entirely and never required. There is no server to gate.
  • To re-share the code mid-session, open the Identity tab in Manage. It’s shown there with a Copy button.

View-Only Mode Online only

The server can serve a second listener on a separate port (default 8081) that is read-only. Observers on this port see live data but cannot create, edit, delete, restore, import, or unload anything.

  • Viewers connect with no access code. The read/write distinction is enforced by the port itself: every POST to the viewer port returns 405.
  • No startup modal on viewer load. Viewers don’t need an identity or workspace selection, so the page paints straight to the report log without prompting.
  • A small orange VIEW ONLY badge sits to the right of the “Workspaces” sidebar header so the operator knows the mode without crowding the report-log header.
  • The form panel is replaced by a persistent Workspaces sidebar on the left, listing every loaded workspace with click-to-switch (local-only: switching here only changes the viewer’s own view, not anyone else’s). Under it, Activity lists the editors’ last changes, as in the Workspaces tab.
  • Each row shows the ATO designator in bold and the workspace title as a subdued sublabel right after it, separated by a dash or a space according to the Title Separator setting.
  • Each row carries up to two small circular indicators next to the workspace title so the viewer can tell their local view apart from the editor’s:
    • A (blue), Active: the workspace viewers are shown, the last one an editor switched to on the main tool. Driven by the server’s config.ato; updates live (no refresh) when an editor switches.
    • S (orange), Showing: the workspace the viewer is currently looking at. Defaults to the active one and follows the editors automatically. Clicking another row pins S there without disturbing A; once the viewer has clicked away, subsequent editor switches no longer drag their view along.
    Hover either indicator for its full label. The A slot is always reserved on every row (invisible where the workspace isn’t active), so the S indicator lands at the same horizontal coordinate on every row instead of shifting position.
  • The right side of each row shows unique reports and total reports counts in the same language the report-log header uses, so the numbers correspond when you switch between views.
  • The source filename (e.g. XG_TEST.txt, Uploaded backup, In-session) is hidden in the viewer sidebar. It’s editor-only metadata about where a workspace came from, irrelevant to observers. The editor’s Workspaces tab in the main report log still shows it.
  • The editor-only Settings items (Horizontal Layout, MGRS Lock, FMV Mode, Select All / Deselect All, Clear Workspace) are hidden, Manage has only its Import / Export tab, and that only exports. Export still works: viewers can download backups and CSVs of what they’re seeing.
  • Live updates flow normally: any edit by an editor appears in the viewer’s table within a fraction of a second, and switching the active workspace on the editor side updates the A indicator on every viewer in real time.
  • The page is stamped read-only by the server before it even loads in the browser, so the editor UI never flashes on a viewer’s refresh.
  • Viewers keep no copy of the reports in browser storage. The page itself comes from the server, so a cached copy would be of no use without it, and it would leave every report at rest on a machine that only watches. Display settings (time mode, title separator) are still remembered.
